# Social Engineering

## 1. Overview

Social engineering attacks manipulate people rather than relying only on technical vulnerabilities. The attacker attempts to influence a victim into revealing information, performing an action, transferring money, granting access, or bypassing a security control.

The central security lesson is that a technically secure system can still be compromised when a trusted person is manipulated.

## 2. Common Techniques

### Phishing
A fraudulent message attempts to make a victim click a link, open an attachment, disclose credentials, or perform another action.

### Spear Phishing
A targeted phishing attack customized for a particular person or organization. Personal or organizational information makes the message more convincing.

### Whaling
A spear-phishing attack directed at a high-value executive or senior decision maker.

### Vishing
Voice-based social engineering, commonly performed through phone calls or voice messages.

### Smishing
Phishing delivered through SMS or other mobile messaging channels.

### Pharming
Redirecting a user to a fraudulent destination even when the user believes they are visiting the correct site. DNS manipulation or compromised hosts can be involved.

### Pretexting
The attacker invents a believable scenario or identity to obtain information or persuade a victim to perform an action.

### Baiting
A tempting object or offer is used to make the victim take an unsafe action. Examples include a supposedly useful download or a found removable drive.

### Quid Pro Quo
The attacker offers a benefit or service in exchange for information or access.

### Tailgating and Piggybacking
An unauthorized person follows an authorized person through a controlled physical entrance. Tailgating generally implies the authorized person is unaware; piggybacking can imply the authorized person knowingly permits entry.

### Shoulder Surfing
Sensitive information is observed directly, such as a password or PIN being entered.

### Dumpster Diving
Discarded documents, media, labels, or devices are examined for useful information.

### Watering-Hole Attack
A site frequently visited by the target group is compromised so that members of the target group encounter malicious content.

### Business Email Compromise
An attacker impersonates or compromises a business account and attempts to cause fraudulent payments, credential disclosure, or sensitive-data transfer.

## 3. Psychological Principles

Attackers frequently exploit authority, urgency, fear, curiosity, trust, familiarity, scarcity, reciprocity, and social pressure. A message saying that an account will be disabled immediately is exploiting urgency and fear; a request apparently coming from a senior executive exploits authority.

## 4. Detection and Mitigation

Use phishing-resistant authentication where practical, email filtering, URL and attachment analysis, user awareness training, verification procedures for financial requests, least privilege, reporting mechanisms, physical access controls, clean-desk practices, and technical controls that prevent users from executing or installing untrusted content.

Never treat training as the only control. Effective defense combines people, process, and technology.

## 5. Security+ Exam Focus

Distinguish the delivery channel and targeting: phishing is broad, spear phishing is targeted, whaling targets executives, vishing uses voice, and smishing uses SMS. Pretexting is a fabricated scenario; baiting uses a tempting lure; tailgating concerns physical entry.

## 6. Key Takeaways

- Social engineering exploits human trust and decision-making.
- Verify unusual requests using an independent communication channel.
- Strong authentication reduces the impact of stolen credentials but does not eliminate social engineering.
- Physical security and cybersecurity are closely connected.
