# Compliance, Auditing, and Assurance

## 1. Compliance

Compliance means meeting applicable legal, regulatory, contractual, and organizational requirements. Requirements may come from governments, industry bodies, customers, contracts, or internal policy.

## 2. Audit

An audit provides structured examination of controls, processes, records, and evidence against defined requirements. An auditor should have sufficient independence and avoid conflicts of interest.

## 3. Internal and External Audits

An internal audit is performed within or on behalf of the organization to evaluate controls and processes. An external audit is performed by an independent outside party and may provide assurance to customers, regulators, or other stakeholders.

## 4. Evidence

Audit evidence should be sufficient, relevant, reliable, and traceable. Examples include configuration records, access reviews, policies, logs, training records, vulnerability reports, change records, and test results.

## 5. Control Assessment

A control may exist on paper but fail operationally. Assurance therefore examines both design and operating effectiveness. Findings should be documented, assigned to owners, prioritized, and tracked to closure.

## 6. Common Framework and Requirement Categories

Security professionals may encounter regulatory requirements, contractual requirements, privacy obligations, security standards, and assurance frameworks. The exact requirement depends on the organization's industry, geography, customers, and data.

## 7. Security+ Exam Focus

Compliance is broader than security. A technically secure organization can still fail a compliance requirement. Audits rely on evidence, while assessments determine whether controls satisfy defined requirements.

## Key Takeaways

Compliance and assurance provide accountability and evidence that security requirements are being addressed. Controls should be continuously monitored rather than prepared only immediately before an audit.
