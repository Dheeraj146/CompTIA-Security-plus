# Security Metrics and Reporting

## 1. Purpose

Security metrics convert security activity and outcomes into information that decision makers can use. Good reporting connects technical measurements to business objectives and risk.

## 2. Useful Metrics

Examples include vulnerability remediation time, percentage of systems meeting secure baselines, patch compliance, phishing-reporting rates, incident response times, recurring incident categories, backup restoration success, control assessment results, and training completion.

## 3. Leading and Lagging Indicators

**Leading indicators** provide signals about conditions that may influence future outcomes, such as unresolved critical vulnerabilities or declining security-training participation.

**Lagging indicators** measure outcomes that have already occurred, such as confirmed incidents or historical losses.

## 4. Key Performance and Risk Indicators

A **KPI** measures performance against an objective. A **KRI** indicates exposure to risk. Both should have clear definitions, owners, data sources, and reporting periods.

## 5. Reporting to Different Audiences

Executives generally need trends, business impact, risk exposure, and decisions requiring support. Technical teams may need detailed event data, affected assets, control status, and remediation tasks. Reports should therefore be tailored to the audience without changing the underlying facts.

## 6. Avoiding Misleading Metrics

A large number of blocked attacks does not necessarily mean security improved; it may simply reflect more attacks or better logging. Metrics should be interpreted with context, baselines, time periods, and business impact.

## 7. Security+ Exam Focus

Metrics should be measurable, relevant, repeatable, and tied to objectives. A metric is useful only when it supports a meaningful security or business decision.

## Key Takeaways

Security reporting should communicate **what happened, why it matters, current exposure, trend, ownership, and what action is required**.
